# Why two analysts value the same company differently

Two analysts can value the same company differently because a valuation is built from a chain of assumptions — about growth, margins, risk, and how long a forecast period should run — and each of those assumptions requires a judgement rather than a direct observation. Different judgements, fed into the same mechanical framework, produce different numbers, even when both analysts are applying the method correctly.

## Where the assumptions enter

A discounted cash flow valuation (covered separately) requires, at minimum, a revenue growth forecast, assumptions about operating margins and reinvestment needs to translate that revenue into free cash flow, a discount rate built from a chosen risk-free rate, beta, and equity risk premium, and a terminal growth rate or exit multiple to value everything beyond the explicit forecast period. None of these inputs is a single, universally agreed figure. Growth forecasts depend on a view of a company's competitive position and its markets going forward. Beta can be measured over different time windows or against different reference indices and will not come out identical each time it's calculated. A terminal growth rate is, by definition, an assumption about the very long run, which is inherently harder to pin down than a near-term forecast.

## How small differences compound

Because a DCF multiplies and discounts these assumptions together across several years, and because terminal value — which typically makes up the majority of the total valuation, as covered separately — is calculated by dividing by the difference between the discount rate and the growth rate, small differences in individual assumptions can produce a large difference in the final output.

## A worked example

Take a company with free cash flow of £68.0 million in the final year of a five-year forecast. Analyst A uses a discount rate of 9% and a terminal growth rate of 3%. Analyst B uses a discount rate of 11% and a terminal growth rate of 2% — both are within a normal range of judgement calls, not extreme outliers.

Analyst A: year-six cash flow = £68.0m × 1.03 = £70.04m. Terminal value = £70.04m ÷ (0.09 − 0.03) = £70.04m ÷ 0.06 = £1,167 million.

Analyst B: year-six cash flow = £68.0m × 1.02 = £69.36m. Terminal value = £69.36m ÷ (0.11 − 0.02) = £69.36m ÷ 0.09 = £771 million.

| | Analyst A | Analyst B |
|---|---|---|
| Discount rate | 9% | 11% |
| Terminal growth rate | 3% | 2% |
| Terminal value | £1,167m | £771m |

A two-percentage-point difference in the discount rate and a one-percentage-point difference in the growth rate — differences that could easily arise from two reasonable people using slightly different inputs for beta or the equity risk premium — produce a terminal value that differs by roughly 50%. Once this flows through to the rest of the valuation, the two analysts' final numbers for the same company, using the same method and the same underlying cash flow forecast, can differ substantially.

## Other sources of difference

Beyond the numbers within a single method, analysts can also choose different methods altogether — one might rely primarily on a DCF, another on comparable company multiples such as EV/EBITDA, and a third on precedent transaction multiples drawn from past acquisitions of similar companies — and each method anchors the valuation to a different reference point. Analysts using comparable multiples can also select different peer companies to compare against, or choose a different point in time to measure those peers' multiples, both of which shift the result. Timing matters too: analysts working from different reporting periods, or normalising for one-off items differently — excluding a litigation charge, for instance, or adjusting for a temporary drop in demand — start from different baseline figures before any forecasting even begins.

## What the disagreement does not mean

When two analysts produce different valuations for the same company, it does not mean one of them made an error the other avoided — both can be applying the same mechanical framework correctly while starting from different, individually defensible assumptions about an uncertain future. A valuation is not a measurement of a fixed, observable quantity in the way a physical measurement is; it's the output of a specific set of judgements, and the gap between two valuations reflects the gap between those judgements, not a gap in either analyst's technique.
