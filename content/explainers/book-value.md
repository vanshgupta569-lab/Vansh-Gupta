# What is book value, and when does it matter

Book value is the value of a company's shareholders' equity as recorded on its balance sheet — total assets minus total liabilities — calculated under accounting rules rather than current market prices. It carries the most information for businesses whose balance sheet assets sit close to their market value, such as banks, insurers, and real estate holders, and the least for businesses whose value lies mainly in things accounting rules leave off the balance sheet, such as brands and technology platforms.

## How book value is calculated

A balance sheet lists everything a company owns (assets, such as cash, inventory, property, and equipment) and everything it owes (liabilities, such as loans, supplier payments due, and other obligations). What's left after liabilities are subtracted from assets is shareholders' equity, which is book value.

Most assets and liabilities on a balance sheet are recorded at historical cost — what was originally paid for them, adjusted over time by depreciation or amortisation for physical and intangible assets — rather than at their current market value. Some items, such as certain financial investments, are instead recorded at fair value and adjusted each period to reflect current prices. Which basis applies depends on the type of asset and the accounting rules being followed.

## A worked example

A company's balance sheet shows total assets of £500 million and total liabilities of £300 million. Book value (shareholders' equity) = £500m − £300m = £200 million.

If the company has 50 million shares outstanding, book value per share = £200m ÷ 50m = £4.00 per share.

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Total assets | £500m |
| Total liabilities | £300m |
| Book value (equity) | £200m |
| Shares outstanding | 50m |
| Book value per share | £4.00 |

If the company's shares trade at £6.00, the price-to-book ratio is £6.00 ÷ £4.00 = 1.5x, meaning the market price sits at one and a half times the accounting value of the equity. A price-to-book ratio close to 1x means the market price and the accounting value of equity are roughly aligned; a ratio further from 1x, in either direction, means the two have diverged, without the ratio itself explaining why.

## Where book value sits closer to market value

For some types of business, the assets on the balance sheet are already close to their current market value, because accounting rules require or permit those specific assets to be revalued regularly. Banks and insurers hold large portfolios of financial assets — loans, securities, investment portfolios — many of which are measured at or close to fair value under accounting standards, so their book value tends to move in closer step with the economic value of what they hold. Real estate and investment holding companies, where the core assets are physical property or stakes in other entities, can show a similar pattern, particularly where those assets are periodically revalued.

## Where book value diverges from market value

For businesses whose value comes largely from things accounting rules don't put on the balance sheet at all — brand recognition, customer relationships, a technology platform, or research and development that was expensed as incurred rather than capitalised as an asset — book value captures only the physical and financial assets that were formally recorded, leaving out a substantial part of what the business is actually worth operating. A company can also carry assets on its books at a historical cost that no longer bears much relation to what those assets are worth today, in either direction, because accounting rules did not require a revaluation. This is why book value tends to carry different weight depending on what sits on a given company's balance sheet: a company whose assets are mostly plant, property, financial instruments, or cash has more of its economic substance reflected in book value than a company whose assets are mostly people, software, and accumulated know-how that accounting standards never capitalised in the first place.

## What book value does not tell you

Book value reflects what was paid for a company's recorded assets, net of liabilities, under the accounting rules that applied at the time — it does not reflect the earnings power of the business, its growth prospects, or the value of anything that accounting standards exclude from the balance sheet entirely. Two companies with identical book value per share can have entirely different capacities to generate future cash flow, and a single book value figure, taken alone, does not indicate which parts of a company's assets are held near current market value and which are recorded at a historical cost that may no longer be closely related to it.
