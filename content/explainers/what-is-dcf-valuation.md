# What is a discounted cash flow valuation

A discounted cash flow (DCF) valuation estimates what a company is worth today by projecting the cash it will generate in future years and converting each of those future amounts into today's money. The output is a single number — the present value of all that future cash — which stands in for the price of owning the business outright.

## Why future cash needs converting

A pound received in five years is not worth the same as a pound received today, even before inflation is considered. Money available now can be invested, redeployed, or held with certainty, while money promised in the future carries the risk that it might not arrive as expected, or might arrive later, or smaller than forecast. A DCF handles this by applying a discount rate — a percentage that shrinks each future cash flow the further out it sits — and the discounted result is called the present value of that cash flow.

## Building the model

A DCF is built in four steps.

First, a forecast period is chosen, typically five to ten years, over which cash flows are projected year by year. Second, each year's free cash flow — the cash a business generates after covering operating costs, tax, and the reinvestment needed to keep running (covered in more detail in a separate article on free cash flow) — is estimated. Third, a discount rate is applied to bring each year's cash flow back to a present value; this rate is usually the company's weighted average cost of capital, or WACC (explained separately). Fourth, because a business is assumed to keep operating beyond the forecast period, a terminal value is calculated to represent all the cash flows expected after the explicit forecast ends, and that terminal value is itself discounted back to the present.

Adding the present value of each forecast year's cash flow to the present value of the terminal value gives the enterprise value — the value of the whole operating business, before splitting out who has a claim on it. Subtracting net debt (total debt minus cash) from enterprise value gives equity value, and dividing equity value by the number of shares outstanding gives a value per share.

## A worked example

Assume a company generates free cash flow of £50 million in year one, growing at 8% a year for five years: £50m, £54m, £58.3m, £63.0m, £68.0m. Using a discount rate of 10%, each year's cash flow is multiplied by a discount factor (1 divided by 1.10 raised to the power of the year number):

| Year | Free cash flow | Discount factor | Present value |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | £50.0m | 0.909 | £45.5m |
| 2 | £54.0m | 0.826 | £44.6m |
| 3 | £58.3m | 0.751 | £43.8m |
| 4 | £63.0m | 0.683 | £43.0m |
| 5 | £68.0m | 0.621 | £42.2m |

These five present values add up to roughly £219 million.

For the terminal value, assume cash flow keeps growing at 3% a year forever after year five. Year six's cash flow is £68.0m × 1.03 = £70.0m. Using the perpetuity growth formula (terminal cash flow divided by the discount rate minus the growth rate), terminal value = £70.0m ÷ (0.10 − 0.03) = £1,000 million. That figure sits at the end of year five, so it is discounted back using the same year-five factor of 0.621, giving a present value of roughly £621 million.

Enterprise value is £219m + £621m = £840 million. If the company carries £100 million of net debt, equity value is £740 million, and if there are 100 million shares outstanding, that works out to £7.40 per share.

## What a DCF does not tell you

A DCF converts assumptions into a number with a great deal of precision, but the precision is borrowed from the assumptions, not generated by the method itself. It does not tell you whether the growth rate, margins, or discount rate used are reasonable — those are judgements fed into the model, and the model has no way of checking them against reality. It does not tell you what the market will pay for the shares tomorrow, since market prices reflect the collective assumptions of everyone trading, which may differ from any one set used here. And because so much of the total value in the example above sits in the terminal value (£621m of the £840m total), a DCF's output is unusually sensitive to assumptions made about a period that has not been explicitly modelled at all — a small change in the growth or discount rate used for that single terminal-value calculation can move the entire valuation by a large margin.
