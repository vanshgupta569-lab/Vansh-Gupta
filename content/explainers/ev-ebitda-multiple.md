# What is EV/EBITDA, and when is it the wrong multiple

EV/EBITDA is a valuation multiple that divides a company's enterprise value by its earnings before interest, tax, depreciation, and amortisation (EBITDA), producing a figure used to compare how the market prices different companies relative to their operating earnings. It becomes a less reliable basis for comparison in situations where EBITDA diverges from the cash a business can actually generate — most notably when capital spending needs, lease structures, or accounting treatments differ significantly between the companies being compared.

## What each part of the ratio measures

Enterprise value (EV) represents the value of a company's entire operating business, regardless of how it is financed. It's calculated as market capitalisation (share price multiplied by shares outstanding) plus total debt, minus cash and cash equivalents. Adding debt and subtracting cash puts companies with different levels of borrowing on a comparable footing, since EV represents what it would cost to acquire the whole business and take on its existing debt.

EBITDA starts from operating profit and adds back depreciation and amortisation — the non-cash charges that spread the cost of past capital spending over time. The idea behind EBITDA is to strip out the effects of financing decisions (interest), tax jurisdiction (tax), and past investment decisions (depreciation and amortisation), leaving a measure of operating performance that is easier to compare across companies with different capital structures, tax rates, and asset ages.

Dividing EV by EBITDA gives a multiple: how many times a company's EBITDA the market is currently pricing the whole business at.

## A worked example

Company A has an enterprise value of £800 million and EBITDA of £100 million, giving an EV/EBITDA multiple of 8x. Company B also has an enterprise value of £800 million and EBITDA of £100 million — the same 8x multiple.

On the multiple alone, the two look identically priced relative to their operating earnings. But suppose Company A requires £10 million of capital expenditure a year to maintain its operations, while Company B requires £40 million. After accounting for that reinvestment, Company A is left with £90 million of EBITDA less capex, while Company B is left with £60 million — a difference the EV/EBITDA multiple, on its own, does not show.

| | Company A | Company B |
|---|---|---|
| Enterprise value | £800m | £800m |
| EBITDA | £100m | £100m |
| EV/EBITDA | 8.0x | 8.0x |
| Annual capex | £10m | £40m |
| EBITDA less capex | £90m | £60m |

## When the multiple stops being comparable

EV/EBITDA becomes harder to use as a like-for-like comparison in several situations, each traceable back to what EBITDA does and doesn't include.

Because EBITDA excludes depreciation and amortisation but not the underlying capital spending that creates those charges, two companies with identical EBITDA but different capital intensity — as in the example above — are not generating the same amount of cash after reinvestment, even at the same multiple.

Because EBITDA sits above the interest line, it doesn't reflect a company's debt burden directly, which is one reason EV (which already incorporates debt) is used in the numerator rather than a profit measure further down the income statement. But this also means the multiple can look similar for a heavily indebted company and a debt-free one, if their operating earnings happen to match, even though their overall financial risk differs.

Because lease accounting rules affect where lease costs sit in a company's financials, and have changed over time and vary between jurisdictions, EBITDA can be calculated on a different basis for companies that structure the same physical assets — a fleet of vehicles, for example — as leases rather than owned assets, or vice versa.

For companies where EBITDA is negative or very close to zero — common at an early stage of a company's growth — the resulting multiple becomes either meaningless, from dividing by a negative or near-zero number, or extremely volatile to small changes in EBITDA. And for banks, insurers, and similar financial companies, interest income and expense are central to the core business rather than a financing cost sitting apart from operations, which is what EBITDA, by stripping out interest, is designed to isolate.

## What EV/EBITDA does not tell you

The multiple tells you how the market prices a company's operating earnings relative to its enterprise value, but on its own it does not tell you how much of that EBITDA converts into cash after the reinvestment needed to sustain the business, how that capital intensity compares with other companies carrying the same multiple, or whether the underlying accounting treatment of leases, R&D, or other items is consistent between the companies being compared.
