# What is free cash flow, and why does it differ from profit

Free cash flow is the cash a business generates from its operations after paying for the capital investment needed to sustain and grow that business. Profit, by contrast, is an accounting measure of income earned during a period, calculated under rules that include non-cash charges and exclude some cash outlays entirely — which is why the two numbers are rarely the same.

## Why profit and cash are different things

Profit is calculated by matching revenue earned in a period against the costs incurred to earn it, following accrual accounting rules. Those rules include charges that never involve a cash payment, such as depreciation and amortisation (the gradual expensing of an asset's cost over its useful life), and they exclude cash that does move but isn't counted as a cost, such as spending on new equipment (capital expenditure, or capex) or cash tied up in unpaid customer invoices. A business can report a solid profit while its cash balance falls, or report a loss while cash in the bank rises, depending on how these non-cash and non-profit items move.

## Getting from profit to free cash flow

The most common route starts at net profit and makes several adjustments.

Depreciation and amortisation are added back, because they reduced profit without ever leaving the bank account. Capital expenditure is then subtracted, because buying equipment or property does leave the bank account, even though it isn't treated as a cost on the income statement in the year it's paid for. The change in net working capital is subtracted — this captures cash tied up in or released from day-to-day operating items such as inventory, amounts owed by customers (receivables), and amounts owed to suppliers (payables). Some versions of the calculation also add back after-tax interest expense, to arrive at free cash flow to the firm rather than free cash flow to equity holders — the difference relates to who has a claim on the cash, lenders or shareholders, a distinction covered in the article on WACC.

## A worked example

Take a company with net profit of £40 million in a given year. Depreciation and amortisation for the year total £15 million, so this is added back: £40m + £15m = £55m. Capital expenditure for the year was £20 million, so this is subtracted: £55m − £20m = £35m. Net working capital increased by £5 million over the year — meaning £5 million of cash became tied up in inventory and receivables, net of any increase in payables — so this is also subtracted: £35m − £5m = £30 million of free cash flow.

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Net profit | £40m |
| Add: depreciation & amortisation | +£15m |
| Less: capital expenditure | −£20m |
| Less: increase in net working capital | −£5m |
| Free cash flow | £30m |

In this example, free cash flow of £30 million is £10 million lower than the reported net profit of £40 million — driven mainly by capital spending that outpaced the non-cash depreciation charge, plus cash absorbed into working capital.

## Why the gap moves around

The size and direction of the gap between profit and free cash flow depends on where a business sits in its investment cycle. A business spending heavily on new capacity will show capex well above its depreciation charge, pulling free cash flow below profit, as in the example above. A business that has finished a period of heavy investment, with depreciation now running ahead of new capex, can show free cash flow above profit. Working capital swings add further movement year to year — a business growing quickly often ties up more cash in inventory and receivables than it releases, while one experiencing a temporary slowdown can release working capital and show a cash inflow unrelated to underlying trading.

## What free cash flow does not tell you

Free cash flow tells you how much cash was generated after the reinvestment a business made during the period, but it does not tell you whether that level of reinvestment is enough to sustain the business going forward, or whether it was unusually high or low compared with a typical year for that company. It does not distinguish cash generated from core operations from cash flows arising from one-off events, such as selling a property or a legal settlement, unless those are separately identified and excluded. And a single year's free cash flow figure says nothing about how it might vary in future years, since both capex and working capital needs can move sharply from one period to the next.
