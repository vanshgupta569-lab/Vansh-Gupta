Financial Modeling Convention Checklist
A line-by-line audit checklist for three-statement models and DCFs, built around the conventions taught in Paul Pignataro's Financial Modeling and Valuation. Every item is written as a general modeling convention in plain language — not a quotation or a reproduction of the book's tables or Walmart worksheets — so it can be checked against any model, not just the book's case study.

A quick structural note before the checklist: a complete model is generally organized as six linked schedules — income statement, cash flow statement, balance sheet, depreciation schedule, working capital schedule, and debt schedule — built roughly in that order, with the debt schedule last because it is the one that closes a circular loop back through the other five. Three years of historical financials followed by a multi-year (commonly five-year) forward projection is a common baseline, not a fixed requirement.

Where the book states a preference or a rule of thumb rather than a hard rule, that is called out explicitly in the item.

Income statement
Revenue is listed by individual stream (or at least reconciled to one) and then summed into a total revenue line, rather than modeled only as a single blended total from the outset.
Historical revenue growth is calculated as a formula referencing the prior and current period's revenue cells, not typed in as a static number.
Projected revenue growth is driven by an explicit, hardcoded growth-rate assumption sitting in its own cell, with the revenue dollar figure itself calculated off that assumption — never the reverse.
The chosen revenue growth assumption is sanity-checked against at least one outside reference point (consensus estimates, management guidance, an investor presentation) before being finalized. Rule of thumb: the book recommends cross-checking against multiple sources rather than mandating any one source.
Before a cost line is projected, it is classified as broadly fixed or variable in nature, and variable costs (most often cost of goods sold) are modeled as a percentage of revenue by default.
The percentage-of-revenue (or equivalent) driver used for a cost line is selected from a defined menu of methods — the average of the last three years, the maximum of the last three years, the minimum, the prior year's figure, or a stated increasing/decreasing trend — and the model or its notes record which method was used and why.
A conservative method is treated as the default when the choice between methods is otherwise close. Rule of thumb, not an absolute — any of the standard methods is considered defensible depending on the evidence.
Depreciation and amortization is confirmed to exist somewhere in the income statement (via a cash-flow-statement or footnote search) even when no D&A line is shown separately, and is then broken out as its own line in the EBITDA-to-EBIT bridge rather than left buried inside cost of goods sold or SG&A.
When D&A is identified as buried inside another expense line, that host expense line is reduced by the D&A amount so D&A can be shown separately without double-counting total expenses.
Where the precise split of a buried depreciation figure between cost of goods sold and SG&A is genuinely unclear, that ambiguity is treated as immaterial as long as EBITDA itself is correctly stated net of depreciation — because EBITDA, not the exact gross-margin breakout, is what the analysis depends on. Rule of thumb.
Other income and expense items (equity in earnings of affiliates, foreign-currency gains/losses, and similar non-operating items) are separated into their own section, with an explicit, documented decision on whether they sit above or below EBIT.
Before linking any interest or other-income line into the EBT build, its historical sign convention is confirmed against the company's own reported figures (some companies show interest income as a negative expense line rather than a positive addition), and that same convention is carried forward consistently.
The income statement follows a strict EBITDA → (less D&A) → EBIT → (less net interest) → EBT → (less taxes) → net income chain, with a margin (percentage of revenue) calculated alongside each subtotal.
Non-recurring or extraordinary items are moved into a separate section below the "clean" (adjusted) net income line, rather than left inside EBITDA, EBIT, or EBT, so the core operating metrics reflect ongoing operations only.
A second, "as-reported" net income line is kept below the non-recurring items and distributions section, so the model can reconcile back to the company's actual reported net income as a sanity check.
Distributions (dividends and non-controlling-interest payouts) are shown as their own section between adjusted net income and reported net income, not silently netted into another line.
Non-recurring items are, by default, not projected forward into future periods, on the reasoning that by definition they are not expected to recur, unless there is specific evidence to the contrary. Rule of thumb.
Basic shares outstanding are sourced from the cover page of the company's most recent filing, not backed into from other disclosures.
Diluted shares outstanding are built using the treasury stock method: in-the-money options and warrants are assumed exercised, the resulting proceeds are assumed used to repurchase shares at the current market price, and only the net new shares (options exercised less shares repurchased) are added to basic shares.
Basic EPS is net income (as reported — i.e., after non-recurring items and distributions) divided by basic shares, and diluted EPS uses the same net-income numerator divided by diluted shares.
Where a company's own EPS convention is ambiguous (whether the net income used includes or excludes non-controlling interests or dividends), the modeler backs into the company's historical reported EPS to confirm which net income figure is actually being used, rather than assuming a standard definition.
Hardcoded inputs and assumption drivers are entered in blue font, and formulas are entered in black font, throughout the income statement.
Cash flow statement
The cash flow statement is organized into three explicit sections — operating, investing, and financing activities — each with its own subtotal, plus a grand total change in cash for the period.
Operating cash flow begins from net income before distributions (before dividends and non-controlling-interest payouts), not from a net income figure taken from further down the income statement.
Every material non-cash income-statement item — D&A, deferred taxes, and other non-cash charges or gains — is added back to net income in the operating section, and the add-back is documented rather than treated as an unexplained plug.
Depreciation and amortization on the cash flow statement is linked from the income statement's D&A line (itself linked from the depreciation schedule), rather than linked a second time directly from the depreciation schedule, so the cash-flow figure is transparently tied to what was actually expensed.
Any cash-flow line item without a dedicated feeder schedule is projected using one of a defined set of methods — the minimum of the last three years (conservative), the maximum (aggressive), the average, the prior year's figure, cyclical repetition, a stated growth trend, or a percentage of a related income-statement or balance-sheet line — and the choice is documented.
Capital expenditures are projected primarily off company guidance where disclosed, cross-checked against the historical capex-as-a-percentage-of-revenue trend, rather than relying on a percent-of-revenue assumption in isolation. Rule of thumb: the book explicitly warns that a percent-of-sales-only capex projection can be too mechanical for a business with lumpy investment needs.
Cash-flow items that depend on discretionary cash availability (discretionary buybacks, discretionary M&A, and similar items) are flagged distinctly and conservatively defaulted (often to zero) pending further research, rather than mechanically extrapolated from history. Rule of thumb.
Dividends are projected as shares outstanding times an assumed dividend per share (sourced from company disclosure where available), not as a percentage of net income or revenue.
Financing-activity debt lines (short-term borrowings, long-term borrowings, capital lease payments) are left unlinked until the debt schedule is complete, then linked from the debt schedule rather than projected independently within the cash flow tab.
Every operating-working-capital line item shown on the cash flow statement is linked from the working capital schedule's year-over-year change (sign-flipped for asset items), not hardcoded or projected independently a second time on the cash flow tab.
Depreciation
Straight-line depreciation is calculated as (asset cost less any residual/scrap value) divided by useful life, with residual value assumed to be zero for a large, pooled asset base unless there is a specific reason to assume otherwise.
An accelerated method (declining balance, sum-of-the-years'-digits, or MACRS) is used specifically when the goal is to model tax-return depreciation rather than GAAP-book depreciation, since it is the gap between an accelerated tax schedule and straight-line book depreciation that creates a deferred tax liability.
The declining-balance rate is (1 ÷ useful life) × an accelerating multiplier (commonly 1.5 or 2.0), applied each period to the shrinking net book value rather than to the original cost.
The sum-of-the-years'-digits rate uses a fixed denominator (the sum of the integers from 1 through the useful life) applied each period to the original asset base, not to a declining balance.
When using MACRS with unknown placed-in-service timing, the mid-quarter, first-quarter convention is used as the default assumption, since it produces the largest first-year depreciation among the standard conventions. Rule of thumb — an explicit stand-in for missing timing information, not a requirement.
The depreciation schedule tracks depreciation of existing net PP&E separately from the depreciation of each future year's incremental capital expenditures, stacking each new capex vintage's own depreciation run beneath the prior vintages rather than blending all capex into one undifferentiated pool.
The beginning balance of net PP&E used in the depreciation schedule is linked from the prior year's ending net PP&E on the balance sheet, not re-hardcoded.
Useful-life assumptions for existing PP&E and for each future year's capex are kept in separate, clearly labeled cells so each can be tuned independently, and the resulting total projected depreciation is checked against the recent historical year-over-year depreciation growth rate as a plausibility test. Rule of thumb: a broad useful-life midpoint is an explicitly acknowledged approximation pending more granular, asset-level data.
A formula that must not shift when copied across periods — most commonly a fixed useful-life assumption divided into a given year's capex every year going forward — is anchored using absolute references, while formulas that should shift period to period are deliberately left unanchored.
A deferred tax liability arising from accelerated tax depreciation versus straight-line book depreciation is quantified as (accelerated depreciation less straight-line depreciation) × the tax rate, and this amount is treated as a non-cash addition in the cash flow statement, reflecting that less cash tax was actually paid than was reported.
A deferred tax asset arising from a net operating loss is tracked through an explicit carryback/carryforward calculation — offsetting the loss first against taxable income in the eligible carryback years, then carrying any remaining balance forward.
Working capital
Operating working capital is defined narrowly as current assets excluding cash and cash equivalents, less current liabilities excluding all interest-bearing debt — not the broader textbook definition of total current assets less total current liabilities.
Only balance sheet items tied directly to day-to-day operations are included (typically receivables, inventory, and prepaid expenses on the asset side; payables, accrued liabilities, and accrued income taxes on the liability side), and each is mapped to a specific, related income-statement driver — receivables to revenue, inventory and payables to cost of goods sold, prepaid expenses and accrued liabilities to SG&A (or its operating-expense equivalent), accrued taxes to the tax expense line.
Non-recurring or discontinued-operations-related current assets and liabilities are excluded from operating working capital, for the same reason they are excluded from operating income-statement metrics.
Historical "days" metrics (days receivable, inventory turnover days, days payable, and similar) are calculated using the average of the current and prior period's balance divided by the related income-statement driver for the current period, multiplied by 360 — not the period-end balance alone — because balance sheet items are point-in-time snapshots while income-statement items are period totals.
Projected working capital balances are driven by holding the relevant days metric constant (or otherwise assumption-driven) and solving forward for the dollar balance — days ÷ 360 × the related projected income-statement driver — rather than growing the prior-period balance by a percentage.
The historical days formula, which uses an averaged balance, is not copied forward into projection periods; a separate, forward-solving formula is used instead, since the projection cannot depend on an average that includes a balance not yet known.
The year-over-year change in each operating-working-capital asset line is linked into the cash flow statement with a sign flip — an increase in the asset shows as a cash outflow (negative), a decrease as a cash inflow (positive).
The year-over-year change in each operating-working-capital liability line is linked into the cash flow statement without a sign flip — an increase in the liability shows as a cash inflow, a decrease as a cash outflow.
Each cash-flow working-capital line is explicitly checked off against its corresponding working-capital-schedule line, with a match check confirming that the sum of the individual changes ties to the total change in operating working capital, so no item is left unlinked or accidentally linked twice.
Balance sheet
Every balance sheet line item is projected by asking two questions: which cash-flow-statement item (or items) drives this balance, and in which direction does that cash flow move the balance.
The model is built so the cash flow statement drives the balance sheet — each period's balance equals the prior period's balance adjusted by the relevant cash-flow change — rather than the reverse approach of deriving the cash flow statement by differencing successive balance sheets. This is the book's stated preference and recommendation, not the only method used in practice; the differencing approach is acknowledged as an alternative with a specific drawback — it can obscure which cash flows (e.g., depreciation versus capex) are actually driving a net change.
For assets other than cash, an increase in the relevant cash-flow driver reduces the balance and a decrease increases it (the same sign convention as working capital); for cash itself, for liabilities, and for equity, the balance moves in the same direction as the cash flow.
Every cash-flow-statement line item used to drive the balance sheet is used exactly once — none are left unlinked to any balance sheet item, and none are linked into more than one balance sheet item.
When a balance sheet fails to balance, the diagnosis is narrowed to one of four defined error types: a cash-flow item omitted from the balance sheet; a cash-flow item linked into the balance sheet more than once; a cash-flow item linked with the wrong sign or pulling from the wrong period's column; or a totaling error within the cash flow statement or the balance sheet itself.
To locate a balancing error systematically, a "differences" column is built showing each balance sheet line's change from the last known-balanced period to the first unbalanced period, and each difference is checked against the matching cash-flow line for both magnitude and direction, with each cash-flow line marked off as it is matched so omissions and duplicates surface directly.
Every formula linking the balance sheet to the cash flow statement follows a consistent structural pattern (prior period's balance, then a plus or minus sign, then the current period's cash-flow driver), so a visual scan of the formulas alone can catch a wrong-direction sign or a reference to the wrong period's column.
Debt schedule
The debt schedule is built last, only once the balance sheet already balances, because it introduces a circular reference that is materially harder to debug on top of an already-unbalanced model.
Every debt instrument appearing on the balance sheet (short-term borrowings, current portion of long-term debt, capital lease obligations due within a year, long-term debt, long-term capital lease obligations, and any others) has a corresponding section in the debt schedule; none are left untracked.
Each debt tranche is modeled with its own beginning balance, issuances/retirements, ending balance, and interest calculation, rather than one blended debt balance, unless there is a specific, stated reason to simplify by combining tranches.
Each tranche's beginning-of-year balance is linked from the prior year's ending balance (from the balance sheet for the first projected year, from the schedule's own prior column thereafter), not re-hardcoded each period.
Issuances and retirements are separated into a mandatory line (scheduled per the debt's contractual terms, hardcoded) and a non-mandatory line (discretionary, cash-availability-driven), kept distinct so an automatic-paydown formula in the non-mandatory row cannot interfere with scheduled contractual payments.
Interest expense on each tranche is calculated on the average of the beginning and ending balance for the period, times an interest rate, rather than on the ending balance alone, to approximate the effect of mid-year issuances or retirements when exact timing is unknown.
Where a single debt line actually represents several sub-tranches at different stated rates, a blended rate is derived (weighted by outstanding principal) and used going forward, rather than applying one tranche's rate to the whole balance.
Total interest expense (and, separately, total issuances/retirements) is calculated as the sum of every individual tranche's corresponding line, and when linking that total into the income statement, any split the company itself discloses (for example, separating term-debt interest from capital-lease interest) is mirrored in how the total is divided, rather than linked as one undifferentiated figure.
"Cash flow before debt paydown" — a supplemental subtotal equal to total change in cash and cash equivalents excluding all debt-related financing cash flows — is calculated on the cash flow statement and fed into the debt schedule as the basis for cash available to service or pay down debt.
A minimum cash cushion is subtracted from cash available to pay down debt, reflecting either a disclosed covenant requirement or an explicit, conservative buffer the modeler has chosen; the size and basis of that cushion are documented rather than left as an unexplained number. The specific cushion amount is a rule of thumb / judgment call, not a formula the book derives mechanically.
Interest income on the projected cash balance is calculated the same way as interest expense — on the average of the beginning and ending cash balance, times an assumed rate — with the rate chosen conservatively absent better information. The specific numeric default used is a rule of thumb, not a fixed requirement.
The debt schedule's own calculated ending cash balance is checked against the balance sheet's cash balance as a final match, confirming that debt issuances/retirements, interest expense, and interest income are all wired correctly.
An automatic (non-mandatory) debt paydown/draw formula is built around a minimum function comparing cash available to the outstanding debt balance — paying down whichever of the two is smaller, and drawing debt automatically when cash available is negative — with an explicit on/off toggle so the automatic logic can be disabled without deleting the formula.
Where a facility has a stated capacity limit (for example, a revolver), the automatic paydown/draw formula caps any drawn amount at that limit using an additional min/max function layered around the base formula.
Circular references
The debt schedule's interaction with interest expense/income, cash, and net income is understood to create an intentional circular reference: interest depends on debt and cash balances, which depend on cash flow before debt paydown, which depends on net income, which depends on interest.
Because the circularity is intentional, Excel's iterative-calculation setting is enabled (rather than treating the circular-reference warning as an error to be engineered away), with a stated maximum number of iterations. A maximum of around 100 iterations is used as a practical default in the book — a rule of thumb, not a required value.
It is understood that once the circular loop exists, a single mistyped or non-numeric cell anywhere inside the loop can cause errors (such as #VALUE!) to propagate through every cell in the loop's path, not just the cell where the mistake originated — so troubleshooting starts by locating and correcting the originating cell and forcing a recalculation, rather than chasing each downstream error individually.
Because errors propagate through the entire circular loop, a correction to a mistyped cell is undone/redone cleanly (for example, via undo) rather than patched piecemeal, to avoid leaving residual inconsistencies elsewhere in the loop.
DCF
A choice is made explicitly between the end-of-year discounting convention (each year's cash flow discounted by a full year — 1, 2, 3, and so on) and the mid-year convention (each year's cash flow discounted a half-year less — 0.5, 1.5, 2.5, and so on), and that choice is applied consistently across every projected period and the terminal value. The choice itself is treated as an analyst's judgment call, not a fixed requirement — the mid-year convention exists specifically to better approximate cash flows arriving throughout the year rather than in one year-end lump sum.
The number of years explicitly projected before the terminal value takes over (commonly five to seven) is treated as a judgment call balancing forecast reliability against how much weight is left resting on the terminal value, not a fixed requirement.
Unlevered free cash flow is built starting from EBIT, not net income, since EBIT is already stated before interest and is therefore closer to a capital-structure-neutral measure of cash flow.
Unlevered free cash flow adds back depreciation and amortization, deferred taxes, and other identified non-cash items; adds the net change in operating working capital taken directly, with its existing sign, from the cash flow statement (rather than re-derived independently from the balance sheet); and subtracts capital expenditures.
Because UFCF starts from EBIT rather than net income, an unlevered tax figure is calculated separately as EBIT × the tax rate and subtracted, rather than pulling the income statement's reported tax expense (which already reflects the tax effect of interest).
Any non-cash or non-recurring adjustment being considered for inclusion in unlevered free cash flow is cross-checked against how the cash flow statement itself treats that same item — whether it is added back at all, and in which section — rather than assumed to belong in UFCF purely by category.
The weighted average cost of capital blends an after-tax cost of debt and a cost of equity, each weighted by their share of total capital measured at market value (not book value), with the debt component tax-effected to reflect the deductibility of interest.
Cost of equity is estimated via the Capital Asset Pricing Model: the risk-free rate, plus beta times the market risk premium.
The risk-free rate, the beta lookback period, and the market risk premium history are all drawn from a consistent tenor (for example, all based on 30-year data or all based on 10-year data), rather than mixed across different horizons. The specific tenor chosen (the book expresses a preference for longer-dated data, reasoning that a DCF represents a business's value through maturity and perpetuity) is a stated preference, not the only defensible choice — a shorter-tenor approach is acknowledged as a reasonable alternative.
When beta is derived from comparable companies rather than the target's own market history, each comparable's beta is unlevered (removing the effect of its own capital structure) before being averaged, and the average is then re-levered using the target company's own capital structure.
The unlevering/relevering formula for beta explicitly incorporates the tax rate and the debt-to-equity ratio, rather than adjusting beta by capital structure informally.
Terminal value is calculated by at least one, and ideally both, of two standard methods — an exit-multiple method (an assumed multiple, commonly EBITDA-based, applied to the final projected year's metric) and a perpetuity-growth method (final-year unlevered free cash flow grown one more year at an assumed perpetual rate, divided by the discount rate less that growth rate) — with both the terminal value and the explicit projection period's cash flows discounted back using the same discount rate and the same year-end/mid-year convention.
The perpetual growth rate used in the perpetuity method is kept low and is grounded in a macro-level proxy such as long-run GDP growth or the rate of inflation, reflecting that it represents growth in perpetuity rather than a near-term forecast. Rule of thumb — the book is explicit that this rate is inherently difficult to pin down precisely.
Where the exit-multiple and perpetuity-growth terminal values diverge materially, that divergence is investigated (as a signal about whether market multiples or the underlying cash-flow projections may be too aggressive or too conservative) rather than silently averaged or ignored.
To go from enterprise value to per-share equity value, net debt (interest-bearing debt less cash), non-controlling interests, and preferred securities are all subtracted from enterprise value before dividing by the diluted share count.
Comparables
Comparable-company metrics are built on a last-twelve-months (LTM) basis using the most recent interim data available — calculated as the most recent full-year figure, plus the interim (quarterly) periods reported since that year-end, minus the same interim periods from the prior year — rather than relying on the most recent fiscal year-end figures alone.
Where a comparable company's fiscal year-end differs from the target's, its financials are calendarized to the target's fiscal period by weighting the overlapping interim periods (by days or by weeks) so the adjusted period matches the target's period as closely as possible.
Calendarization and LTM adjustments are made using the most granular data available — quarterly rather than only annual, and day-weighted rather than month-weighted where fiscal periods don't align cleanly — because finer granularity better captures seasonality in the underlying business. Rule of thumb: a coarser adjustment is acknowledged as acceptable when finer data simply isn't available.
A comparable company's non-recurring or extraordinary items are identified and reclassified out of EBITDA/EBIT using the same standard applied to the target company, so the definition of "core operating" is consistent across the whole comp set rather than company-specific.
When a non-recurring item is moved below the operating lines for a comparable company, its associated tax effect is adjusted at the tax rate (net of tax) rather than moved at its gross, pre-tax amount.
Where it is genuinely unclear which expense line contains a comparable company's depreciation, the precise allocation between cost of goods sold and SG&A is treated as immaterial as long as the resulting EBITDA is correctly stated net of that depreciation. Rule of thumb.
A missing quarter (commonly the final quarter of a fiscal year, when only annual and interim data are separately reported) is derived for flow items — revenue, expenses, and similar dollar figures — by subtracting the sum of the known interim periods from the full-year total.
Derived rate or ratio figures (such as an implied tax rate) for that same missing quarter are recalculated independently from their own derived dollar components, not obtained by the same subtraction method used for flow items, since a rate is not additive across periods the way a dollar flow is.
Share count is treated as a point-in-time balance rather than a flow: a missing quarter's share count is taken as the period-end balance itself, not backed into by subtracting other quarters' share counts.
Diluted share count for each comparable company is rebuilt using the same treasury-stock-method mechanics used for the target company (in-the-money options assumed exercised, proceeds assumed used to repurchase shares at the current market price, net new shares added to basic shares), rather than taken as a reported figure without independent verification.
Enterprise value for each comparable is built from market value of equity (diluted shares × current share price) plus all interest-bearing debt (short- and long-term, including capital leases and convertible debt), preferred securities, and non-controlling/minority interest, less cash and cash equivalents, with any material off-balance-sheet obligations researched and considered for inclusion.
Multiples pair the correct value base with the correct financial metric: metrics stated after interest expense (net income, EPS) are paired with market value of equity; metrics stated before interest expense (revenue, EBIT, EBITDA) are paired with enterprise value.
Each multiple is calculated on the same three time bases across the entire comparable set (most recent fiscal year-end, LTM, and next-year projected), so that summary statistics such as median, high, and low compare like periods across companies rather than mixing bases.
Formula discipline
A cell reference that must not shift when a formula is copied across columns or down rows is anchored with an absolute reference (the dollar-sign convention on the column, the row, or both as needed), and a reference that should shift with the copy is deliberately left unanchored — the choice is made intentionally in each case, not left to whatever the spreadsheet defaults to.
Formulas locate the cells they need by direct cell reference (or named range), not by visually counting rows or columns and hardcoding an implied position that would silently break if a row were later inserted or deleted elsewhere in the sheet.
Every hardcoded number and assumption driver is entered in blue font, and every formula (anything derived from other cells) is entered in black font, consistently across the entire model — not just the income statement.
A formula that could technically be copied across periods but must actually hold a distinct, period-specific assumption (for example, a different useful life applied to each year's own incremental capital expenditure) is given its own separate, clearly labeled input cell for that period, rather than copied forward from an adjacent period's formula.
Judgment calls made in constructing a line item — an assumption about where an expense is buried, a decision to reclassify an item as non-recurring, the projection method chosen for an ambiguous line — are documented in the model itself (for example, via a cell comment or footnote), so a reviewer can see why a particular number was chosen rather than only what the number is.
A note on scope: this checklist covers modeling mechanics and conventions — the "how" of building and auditing a model. It does not reproduce the book's specific Walmart figures, its exhibit tables, or its exact keystroke-by-keystroke Excel instructions, since those are the book's particular expression rather than general modeling method. Nothing in the source material required leaving out any substantive convention to honor that distinction.
